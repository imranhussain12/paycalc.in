What Is the Fitment Factor?

Updated August 2026 By the PayCalc.in team · Sources cited throughout

One number converts the entire government pay structure from old to new. Here is how it is built, why 2.57 was chosen last time, and what 2.86 or 3.00 would mean for you.

The definition

The fitment factor is the uniform multiplier a pay commission applies to every employee's existing basic pay to produce the new basic pay: new basic = old basic × fitment factor, then rounded into the nearest cell of the new pay matrix. One factor, all 18 levels, no exceptions.

Anatomy of the factor: DA merger + real increase

The factor has two components:

  1. DA neutralisation. On the changeover date the accumulated DA merges into basic pay. If DA is 60%, a factor of 1.60 merely keeps your Basic + DA unchanged.
  2. Real increase. Whatever the commission grants above neutralisation is the genuine raise.

The 7th CPC's arithmetic, from its own report: DA on 1 January 2016 was 125%, requiring 2.25 to neutralise; the commission granted a real rise of 14.29%, so 2.25 × 1.1429 ≈ 2.57 (7th CPC Report).

Apply the same anatomy to the 8th CPC with 60% DA expected on 1 January 2026:

This is why some economists call 2.86 ambitious: it implies a real raise far above the 7th CPC's 14.3%. It is also why comparing raw factors across commissions misleads — 2.57 absorbed 125 points of DA; the next factor only absorbs 60.

Deriving the factor from first principles

A pay commission does not pick the factor from the air. It works from the minimum wage upward, and the factor falls out as a ratio.

The 7th CPC's own chain of reasoning: it computed a needs-based minimum wage using the Aykroyd formula and arrived at ₹18,000 a month. The existing minimum was ₹7,000 basic plus 125% DA, equal to ₹15,750. Dividing the new minimum by the old basic — ₹18,000 ÷ ₹7,000 — gives 2.57. The factor is therefore a consequence of the minimum wage decision, not an independent choice.

This matters for predicting the 8th CPC. If the commission sets a new minimum of ₹51,480, the factor is 2.86. If it sets ₹46,260, the factor is 2.57. Arguing about the factor and arguing about the minimum wage are the same argument.

Separating neutralisation from the real raise

Any factor splits into two parts: the portion that merely replaces the DA you already draw, and the portion that is a genuine increase.

Factor = (1 + DA rate) × (1 + real increase)

At the 7th CPC changeover, DA was 125%, so neutralisation alone required 2.25. The commission granted a real increase of 14.29%, and 2.25 × 1.1429 gives 2.57.

Applying the same decomposition to 2026, with DA at 60%, neutralisation requires only 1.60:

Read that table carefully, because it reframes the debate. A factor of 2.57 would not be "the same as last time" — it would be four times the real increase the 7th CPC granted, simply because there is far less DA to absorb. This is the single most misunderstood point in public discussion of the 8th CPC.

The factor applies to pensions too

Pension revision has followed pay revision at every changeover. At the 7th CPC, existing pensioners received the better of two options: their basic pension multiplied by 2.57, or a notional re-fixation based on the level and stage at which they retired, mapped into the new matrix.

The second option was introduced to address pension parity — the problem of an officer who retired in 1990 drawing far less than one who retired from the same post in 2015. Expect the 8th CPC to face the same question, and expect the same two-option approach. Model your own revision in the pension calculator.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fitment factor in simple words?

It is the single multiplier applied to your current basic pay to get your new basic pay when a pay commission takes effect. If the factor is 2.86 and your basic is ₹50,000, your new basic is ₹1,43,000.

Why was the 7th CPC factor 2.57?

On 1 January 2016 DA stood at 125%. Merging that DA alone required a factor of 2.25 (basic × 2.25 = basic + 125% DA). The commission then granted a real increase of about 14.3%, giving 2.25 × 1.143 ≈ 2.57.

What factor do unions demand for the 8th CPC?

The National Council (JCM) Staff Side has demanded a minimum of 2.86, arguing the minimum pay should rise from ₹18,000 to at least ₹51,480.

Does a bigger fitment factor mean a proportionally bigger raise?

Not proportionally, because part of the factor only replaces the DA you already receive. At 60% DA, a factor of 1.60 would mean zero real increase. Every point above 1.60 is real gain.

Is the fitment factor the same for all levels?

Yes — one uniform factor applies to every level, which is why it is politically so contested. The 6th CPC briefly had differential multiples, but uniformity has been the rule since.

Where does the fitment factor number actually come from?

It is derived, not chosen. The commission determines a new minimum wage, then divides it by the existing minimum basic pay. The 7th CPC set ₹18,000 against an existing ₹7,000, giving 2.57. Arguing about the factor is therefore the same as arguing about the minimum wage.

Would a factor of 2.57 mean the same raise as the 7th CPC?

No — it would be far larger in real terms. In 2016 DA stood at 125%, so 2.25 of that 2.57 was pure neutralisation and the real raise was 14.3%. In 2026 DA is around 60%, needing only 1.60 to neutralise, so 2.57 would imply a real increase of about 61%.

Does the fitment factor apply to pensioners as well?

Yes. At the 7th CPC, pensioners received the higher of two options: existing basic pension multiplied by 2.57, or notional re-fixation in the new matrix based on the level and stage at retirement. The 8th CPC is expected to follow a similar approach.

Is the fitment factor the same for every pay level?

Yes, one uniform factor applies across all levels. The 7th CPC made a single exception at Level 13, applying 2.67 to correct a Grade Pay 8700 anomaly.
